                                         Abstract


Knowledge production is cumulative: publications, datasets, methods, software, and
other intellectual resources depend on prior work. Yet when one resource is found
unreliable, when should the knowledge products that depends on it be treated? The
scholarly communication system has only begun to grapple with regulation on the
grounds of dependencies, such as citations to retracted articles and publications utilizing
improper datasets, and we often found it lacking clear criteria for making such judgments
and falling short in explaining the decisions to affected authors and the public.
   This dissertation focuses on the issue of constructing proper justifications for
scrutinizing or sanctioning an entity that depends on another entity known to be
unreliable. We grounded our investigation in understanding the notion of unreliability
propagation: the circumstances where the unreliability of one entity leads to concern
about another. We conducted a conceptual analysis to investigate the individually
necessary and jointly sufficient conditions for unreliability to propagate between entities,
regardless of their type. We also curated a dataset of 284 publications citing a widely
used computational chemistry protocol affected by a code glitch, with expert annotations
of their risk of unreliability propagation and supporting reasoning. Drawing from the
conceptual analysis and the annotation, we formulated the process of constructing
justifications as defeasible reasoning and developed computational pipelines that generate
risk assessments with justifications expressed in natural language and formal logic.
   This dissertation contributes to information science an understanding of how trust to
information-bearing objects is interconnected. It also contributes a principled
methodology to assess unreliability propagation and make decisions about scrutinizing or
sanctioning dependent knowledge products more explicit, communicable, and open to
debate.

1.1 Background and motivations

Consider the following cases:

  1. An article that provided key evidence for a leading theory of Alzheimer’s disease
     was retracted 18 years after publication, following the discovery of excessive image
     manipulation within it (Lesné et al., 2006). By then this article had already played
     a significant role in shaping subsequent research directions, which failed to produce
     any effective treatment for the disease (Piller, 2025).

  2. Two medical professionals wrote a five-sentence letter to the New England Journal
     of Medicine to report that only four patients out of 11,882 patients received
     “narcotic preparation” developed addiction (Porter & Jick, 1980). This letter was
     later widely (mis)cited to support the claim that addictions among patients who
     underwent long-term opioids treatment are rare (Leung et al., 2017), despite the
     fact that the letter does not ascertain the claim and is not a proper medical
     evidence. This five-sentence letter remains available on the New England Journal of
     Medicine website, accompanied by an Editor’s Note acknowledging its unfortunate
     role in the opioid crisis.

  3. A lab has been using a cell line that the researchers believed to be intestinal cells.
     Yet unbeknownst to them, the cell line have been replaced by HeLa cell, an
     aggressive cervical cancer cell. Under this mistaken assumption, the researchers
     published approximately 40 papers and also distributed the misidentified cell line to
     other laboratories (Capes-Davis et al., 2010).

  4. A group of organic chemists wrote a protocol to teach their fellow organic chemists
     a useful technique: using sophisticated computational chemistry methods to assign
     chemical structures to newly discovered or synthesized organic

                                              6
        compounds (Willoughby et al., 2014). The protocol was well written and well
        received. Yet the authors were not defensive enough in coding one of their scripts,
        whose flaw was discovered by another group of organic chemists 5 years after the
        protocol’s publication (Neupane et al., 2019). The validity of the more than 100
        studies citing the protocol was questioned (Bender, 2019).

  5. A retired engineer constructed a facial-image dataset to develop a machine-learning
        tool for screening children for autism. He collected images from autism-related
        websites and Facebook pages without verifying the diagnostic status of the children
        included. Neither did he obtained consent from their guardians. The dataset was
        subsequently used to train models in many studies. Five publications had been
        retracted for both ethical and reliability concerns (Alkhawaldeh et al., 2025; Ding
        et al., 2025; Mahmood et al., 2025; Talaat et al., 2024; Vasant Bidwe et al., 2024),
        with more slated for investigation and retraction (McMurray, 2025).

   My colleagues and I coined the term “unreliability propagation” (UP) for those
cases (Zheng, Fu, Sarol, et al., 2024). Unreliability propagation arises from epistemic
reliance: an epistemic agent delegates a crucial part of their intellectual work to other
agents, which comes with a price if their trust was misplaced. In Cases 1 and 2, patients
and the public entrusted scientists and medical professionals to develop safe and effective
treatments. In Cases 3, 4, and 5, researchers relied on research resources produced by
others as inputs to their own experiments. When an unreliable participant—whether
human or non-human—occupies an influential position in systems of knowledge
production, its unreliability can propagate outward, with far-reaching consequences for
scientific research and society.
   Responsibility for the propagated unreliability does not rest solely with the source. In
Cases 1 and 2, participants in the knowledge production and dissemination system
accepted and acted on the claims despite their weak evidential foundations.1 . In Case 3,

    As information professionals, we bear a responsibility to protect the integrity of
information ecosystems from unreliability propagation. This responsibility motivates the
dissertation. The dissertation focuses on one critical issue: constructing proper
justifications for scrutinizing or sanctioning an entity that depends on another entity
known to be unreliable, whether it is a scientific publication, dataset, piece of software, or
other intellectual resource. This focus emerged in part from observing disparities in how
such dependent entities have been treated. In Case 5, five publications were retracted on
the grounds of both their reliance on the unreliable dataset and ethical concerns, and
every retraction note stated that the authors disagreed with the retractions. In contrast,
many of the dependent entities implicated in Cases 1–4 remained in the body of scientific
literature without comparable sanctions. These disparities raise the question of whether
the different actions taken in these cases are justified and on what grounds.
    The two research projects in this dissertation tackle the problem of justification from
different angles. The first is based on an empirical case of a widely used computational
chemistry protocol containing a code glitch. It investigates how to computationally assess
dependent publications according to their risk of propagating the protocol’s unreliability
and most importantly, generate explanations (used as justifications) for these risk
assessments. The second seeks to identify the conditions under which unreliability has
propagated from an entity 𝑋 to another entity 𝑌 , irrespective of their types. By
specifying the conditions under which 𝑌 is itself unreliable because of its dependence on
𝑋, the analysis provides theoretical grounds for determining when sanction is justified.

1.2 Research questions and approaches

1.2.1 RQ1: What are the individually necessary and jointly sufficient
        conditions for unreliability to propagate from entity 𝑥 to entity 𝑦?

We undertake a more rigorous theoretical examination of unreliability propagation. We
used the philosophical method of analysis (Beaney & Raysmith, 2024), specifically
analysis-by-decomposition and analysis-by-transformation (Beaney & Raysmith, 2024).
The former is used to guide the generation of conditions for unreliability propagation. We
then drew on theories of conditional logic to give a more rigorous interpretation of the
conditions initially formulated in natural language. More details about this methodology
will be covered in Chapter 3. By establishing when unreliability has propagated from 𝑋
to 𝑌 , this analysis provides the justification for sanctioning 𝑌 as unreliable.


1.2.2 RQ2: Given the insight generated from RQ1, how would we design a
        method to triage publications citing an unreliable source according to
        their risk of propagating unreliability and provide justifications?

We adopted an ‘expert-in-the-loop” approach, with the author serving as one of the
domain experts and as the designer of the computational methods. The process began
with annotation: we annotated 294 publications citing the computational chemistry
protocol from Case 4 (Willoughby et al., 2014) as either ‘at risk” or “not at risk” of
propagating the protocol’s unreliability. Through the annotation process, we developed a
conceptual model of propagation-risk assessment as a process of defeasible
reasoning (Pollock, 1987). Based on this model, we designed computational methods to
assess propagation risk and provide justifications for why particular dependent
publications warrant further scrutiny. We evaluated these methods against the
annotations.

2.1 Cases that inspired the term “unreliability propagation”

Currently, we use the term “unreliability propagation” as an umbrella term for a group of
related cases. Previous work has used terms such as error propagation (Thomsen &
Resnik, 1995) and error cascade (Bortolus, 2008). The limitation of this error-centered
terminology is that, in many cases, the source is unreliable rather than erroneous, as the
cases reviewed below will show. We now turn to these cases.
   One of the most prominent and extensively studied cases is the continued citation of
retracted publications. Negative citations to retracted publications can help correct the
scientific record, but positive or affirmative citations dominate quantitatively (Budd
et al., 2016; Hsiao & Schneider, 2021; Pfeifer & Snodgrass, 1990). Positive citations may
remain appropriate when they refer to content unaffected by the grounds for retraction.
However, given current practices for communicating and managing retractions,
determining which content remains unaffected can be difficult (Barbour et al., 2009;
Fanelli et al., 2018). Positive citations that rely on invalidated content may propagate
unreliability. In one taxonomy regarding citations to retracted publications, Budd et al.
(2016) distinguished “tacit positive citations” from “substantive positive citations”
among citations to retracted papers. Tacit citations mention papers as part of a
literature review, whereas substantive citations refer to papers “for employment as
methodology, affirmation of results, or other usage that signals a genuine usage of the
retracted paper.” This taxonomy reflects certain intuitions but falls short in logical
clarity. Most obviously, it excludes the possibility that citing the retracted paper in the
literature review constitutes “genuine use.” Second, it singles out employment as
methodology and affirmation of results as types of genuine use of the retracted paper
without clarifying what “genuine use” is. Our earlier work offers a more precise account
of “genuine use” through argumentation-based curation models (Fu & Schneider, 2025).



                                             11
Under this account, we could say that retracted research is genuinely used when it
supports key argumentative components. Based on the particular ontology we chose to
use, the micro-publication model (Clark et al., 2014), citations that support methods,
materials, or key steps in inference are more consequential.
    Another prominent and well-studied case is misidentified cell lines (American Type
Culture Collection Standards Development Organization Workgroup ASN-0002, 2010).
Misidentified cell lines are cell lines believed to have one identity (e.g., intestinal cells)
but that in fact have a different identity (e.g., cervical cancer cells). A misidentified cell
line is an unreliable model of the biological system it is supposed to represent. When
researchers examines a different phenomenon from the one they intend to study due to
the unreliability of the cell line, it creates a material error (Allchin, 2001) that
fundamentally severs the relationship between resulting data and phenomenon. Research
conducted on misidentified cell lines could be regarded as wasted (Korch & Capes-Davis,
2021). Yet, for greater precision, one must establish that the misidentified cell lines were
actually used under their mistaken identities (Vaughan et al., 2017). Even when they
were, some argue that the research can still retain scientific merit as long as the cell line’s
identity was not “targeted” in the research (Gorphe, 2019). Some have also argued that
studies based on misidentified cell lines can nevertheless be repurposed. Rae et al. (2007)
established that a cell line MDA-MB-435 is not a breast cancer cell line but a melanoma
cell line. Yet rather than treating everything done with MDA-MB-435 as wasted, they
explicitly argued that the cell line remained valuable for studying cancer metastasis and
that the large body of prior MDA-MB-435 research could become “a valuable new
resource for the melanoma research community.”

in the biological sciences. Here, bad taxonomy refers to the identification, classification,

depends on ecological studies, while ecological studies depend on taxonomy. This
structure allows a taxonomic error to be assimilated into ecological studies and
subsequently into environmental management. Second, Bortolus showed that the
consequences of such propagation vary. A taxonomic error can remain relatively harmless
in some cases but compromises the environment in others. Bortolus grounds this
progression of severity in the core objectives of the biological sciences: understanding
patterns and processes in nature and, ultimately, manipulating natural resources in
beneficial and optimal ways. Several concrete examples illustrate the progression. In one

   A prominent class of unreliability propagation that has emerged in recent years were
unreliable datasets used to train machine learning models. We observe two types of
situations, depending on whether propagation can be established. Poore et al. (2020)
used a computational pipeline to transform existing cancer-related sequencing data into a
new dataset, which was later reused by several studies (Gihawi et al., 2023). Subsequent
reanalysis identified serious problems in the pipeline that generated this new
dataset (Gihawi et al., 2023), and the Poore et al. article was eventually retracted (Poore
et al., 2024). One downstream study by Hermida et al. (2022) had used the processed
data from Poore et al. as input for its own models. After the Poore retraction, the
authors reanalyzed their study. They could not reproduce their original findings and
voluntarily retracted the article (Hermida et al., 2025). All authors agreed to this
retraction. In this case, UP can be established more directly because of the possibly to
run a analysis with dataset replacement.
   Such direct comparison and verification is not always possible. An autism
facial-recognition dataset (Piosenka, n.d.-a, n.d.-b) was constructed using images
downloaded from autism-related websites and Facebook pages. Class labels were assigned
based on inferred rather than verified diagnoses, and the images were used without
consent from the children’s guardians. By the time these problems were recognized, the
dataset had been used in more than 90 published studies (McMurray, 2025). Publishers
began investigations and retractions based on concerns about both data reliability and


                                             14
research ethics, but most authors disagreed with the retractions (Alkhawaldeh et al.,
2025; Ding et al., 2025; Mahmood et al., 2025; Talaat et al., 2024; Vasant Bidwe et al.,
2024). Similar concerns surround two Kaggle datasets used for stroke and diabetes
prediction (Fedesoriano, n.d.; Mustafa, n.d.). These datasets lack clear provenance and
certain variables display abnormal patterns. Gibson et al. (2026) identified 125 studies
using them, and several of which were later retracted. Similar to the autism
facial-recognition case, the editors stated their lost of confidence in the reliability of the
findings in the retraction notice (interpreted as the reason for reaction) while the authors
either do not respond or disagree with the decisions (El-Geneedy et al., 2026; Hassan
et al., 2026; J M & P, 2026; Karpagam et al., 2026; Mochurad et al., 2026; Saleem et al.,
2026; Zhou et al., 2026). Admittedly, in these cases, UP cannot be verified in the same
way as in the Poore–Hermida case because we will have no way to “correct” the datasets,
which could potentially explain the reaction from the authors. Yet, on the other hand,
publishers’ decision could be justified as the lack of sufficient epistemic standing for the
underlying data can itself warrant retractions.


2.2 Policies and measures to address unreliability propagation

Policies and measures applied to retracted scientific publications                Major
citation management software packages now routinely flag retracted publications (Price,
2021). Many scholarly publication sites display a Crossmark button next to a publication
to allow readers to check retraction status (Meyer, 2011). Those measures helps prevent
unreliability propagation proactively. The Cochrane Collaboration, an organization
produces and stewards trusted systematic reviews for healthcare, mandates the
withdrawal a review if its conclusions “are unreliable as a direct result of the retraction of
an included study” (Cochrane Collaboration, 2026). Committee on Publication Ethics or
COPE’s Guidelines on Retracted Articles dictates that “[a]rticles that relied on
subsequently retracted articles in reaching their own conclusions, such as systematic


                                              15
reviews or meta-analyses, may themselves need to be corrected or retracted” (Barbour
et al., 2009).


Policies and measures applied to misidentified cell lines After years of effort,
currently, there is a relatively mature infrastructure to prevent unreliability propagation
of misidentified cell lines. This infrastructure encompasses misidentification warning,
authentication standards, pre-use requirements, pre-publication requirements, and
post-publication responses (Weiskirchen, 2026). Table 2.1 provides examples of those
policies and measures. Notably, to our knowledge, there is no specific policy that governs
how journals should handle published articles found to have used misidentified cell lines.
Weiskirchen (2025) documented four cases in which concerns were reported to journals
about publications using misidentified cell lines, with varied outcomes. One journal
responded promptly by sending the commentary for peer review and subsequently
publishing it. Another journal took a similar approach and also invited the authors of
the original paper to respond. A third journal declined to publish the commentary but
launched a private investigation. A fourth journal rejected the commentary and did not
respond to subsequent follow-up attempts.


Policies to AI systems depending on unreliable datasets While we are not
aware of any policies targeting publications that depend on unreliable data in scholarly

2.3 The idea of reliability

2.3.1 Reliability grounded in Reliance

At the most fundamental level, reliability concerns whether something can be relied upon
by someone and is a concept grounded in reliance. A subject 𝑆 relies on 𝑋 to 𝜑 when 𝑆
is prepared to act on the supposition that 𝑋 will 𝜑 (Goldberg, 2020; Holton, 1994). 𝑋
can be a person, artifact, or natural process and 𝜑 can be an action, behavior, or process.
Reliability is thus the property of 𝑋 that makes 𝑋 suitable for the reliance that 𝑆 places
on it, while unreliability is the lack of that property. For example, misidentified cell lines
were unreliable because they lacked the biological identity required to serve as suitable
experimental models of the intended biological systems. In systematic reviews, retracted
studies are unreliable because their findings lack the epistemic standing that would make
them suitable for use as evidence.
   One notable point about this fundamental sense of reliance is its close relation to the
concept of trust. Philosophers often treat trust as a form of reliance with additional
conditions, which means that trustworthiness cannot simply replace reliability for our
purposes. Those additional conditions include participant stance (Holton, 1994), good
will from the trustee (Baier, 1986), affective attitude (Jones, 1996), perceived
commitment (Hawley, 2014). One important difference is that when trust is violated, a
sense of betrayal or resentment on the side of the trustee is appropriate, whereas the
failure of mere reliance does not warrant such reactions (Baier, 1986; Hawley, 2014). A
biomedical researcher who discovers that a cell line has been misidentified may regard the
cell line as unreliable, but the researcher is unlikely to feel betrayed by the cell line.
   An important distinction that has become salient in recent discussions of trust in AI
and ML is that between trust and appropriate reliance (Benda et al., 2022; Ryan, 2020).
Some philosophers argue that applying the concept of trust directly to AI is inappropriate
because trust involves interpersonal features (Ryan, 2020). On these accounts, what is at



                                              21
issue instead is whether reliance on such systems is appropriate (Benda et al., 2022; Lee
& See, 2004). For example, a responsible physician may rely on an AI diagnostic system
when there are adequate grounds for regarding the system as reliable for its intended use,
but this does not mean that the physician trusts the system.


2.3.2 Reliability as in the Reliabilist epistemology

Reliabilist epistemology develops a specialized epistemic application of this general
notion of reliability. Instead of:


                                     𝑆 relies on 𝑋 to 𝜑.

   We have:


           𝑆 relies on a belief formation process 𝑋 to produce true beliefs (𝜑).

   This leads to an account of reliability in terms of truth-conduciveness: a reliable
process tends to yield true rather than false beliefs (Goldman, 1979). However, there are
different ways to understand truth-conduciveness. On a frequency account, reliability
concerns the proportion of true beliefs produced by actual instances of the process; on a
propensity account, reliability concerns the proportion of true beliefs that would be
produced across a suitable range of possible instances (Alston, 1995; Goldman, 1979).
The propensity account has been favored, in part, because it accommodates problems
such as single-case reliability (Alston, 1995; Feldman, 1985). However, the frequency
account provides the actual determination of propensity: observing the frequency of true
outcomes is the most directly way to assess reliability (Alston, 1995). Applied to
computational circumstances, Durán (2026) argues that reliability of algorithms is
determined by reliability indicators generated from formal methods, algorithmic metrics,
expert competencies, research cultures, and other scientific practices.

than false beliefs (Goldman, 1979). This leaves open how truth-conduciveness should be
understood: in terms of the observed frequency of true beliefs, or in terms of what the
process would produce across actual and relevant possible circumstances.

2.6 Models of justification

In this dissertation, a reason refers to a consideration that bears on whether a conclusion
should be accepted. We use the term in its informal sense, without committing to
particular formal representations. A justification thus refers to one or more reasons in
favor of a conclusion that are recognized as justified under certain norms. We describe
what scholars think justification is in their models without engaging in deeper
philosophical discussions about justification, for example, the debate over what makes a
belief justified.
   What is the simplest justification? In its simplest form, we could say that a reason
supports a conclusion. Toulmin provided more structural clarity by using a warrant to
license the move from reason to conclusion and using backing to support the warrant
itself (Toulmin, 2003).
   Such support needs not be conclusive. In defeasible reasoning, conclusions drawn
under incomplete information may have to be withdrawn when new information becomes
available. Pollock proposed the idea of the prima facie reason, a logical reason for
someone to believe something, yet for which there exist defeaters (Pollock, 1974).
   He thus developed a theory around defeasible justification (Pollock, 1974, 1987, 2001).
In his view, a prima facie reason has justificatory forces without having to be conclusive
or free of defeaters (Pollock, 1974).
   In artificial intelligence research, defeasibility was studied under the notion of
non-monotonic reasoning. Several forms of non-monotonic logic were developed to


                                             26
formalize the features of defeasibility. Reiter’s default logic models defeasibility through
the applicability of default rules: rules whose applicability can be canceled when new
information emerges (Reiter, 1980). McDermott and Doyle locate defeasibility in the
assumptions used to license an inference: new information can invalidate those
assumptions, thereby blocking an inference that was previously
available (mcdermott_nonmonotonic_1980). McCarthy takes a different approach
via abnormalities. His approach assumes a case is normal unless the available information
shows otherwise. When new information establishes an abnormality, the ordinary
conclusion may no longer follow (McCarthy, 1980).
   Pollock himself makes defeaters explicit in the model (Pollock, 1987). A prima facie
reason provides justification to a conclusion that can be defeated by additional
information. Pollock distinguishes two kinds of defeaters: a rebutting defeater is a reason
for an opposing conclusion, whereas an undercutting defeater attacks the connection
between a reason and its conclusion (Pollock, 1987).
   Varies about defeasibility were further developed within the broader field of formal
argumentation, which considers interactions between multiple reasons (or argument).
Dung’s abstract argumentation framework shifts the unit of analysis from reasons and
inferences to the whole argument, abstracting out its internal structure and focusing
instead on adversarial relations among arguments (Dung, 1995). Under Dung’s
framework, justification is a property ascribed by membership: an argument is justified if
it is a member of the unique extension under the grounded semantics (most stringent) or
a member of every extension under the preferred semantics (less stringent) (Prakken,
2005).
   Later work in structured argumentation brought the internal structure of arguments
back into view. Combining Pollock’s distinction between rebutting and undercutting
defeaters and Vreeswijk’s premise attack, Prakken distinguishes attacks on a premise
(undermining), on conclusion (rebutting), and on the inference (undercutting) (Prakken,


                                             27
2010). Prakken further distinguishes attack from defeat: an attack is a conflict between
arguments, whereas defeat adds an evaluative judgment about whether that attack
succeeds. The evaluation is done primarily through a preference order. The resulting
defeat relation is then evaluated under Dung-style semantics, which will provide the
justificatory status of the arguments.
   In contrast to the formal approaches reviewed so far, Walton developed a theory of
argumentation schemes that offers a more socially- and dialogically-grounded perspective
on defeasible justification. Walton and his collaborators systematically identified and
classified recurring patterns of reasoning found in everyday discourse and various
domains of practice, such as arguments from expert opinion, analogy, consequences, and
practical reasoning (walton_argumentation_1996;
walton_argumentation_2008). These argumentation schemes represent familiar
ways in which people provide reasons for accepting conclusions, even when such reasons
are not conclusive. In this respect, they closely resemble Pollock’s prima facie inference
rules (prakken_nature_2010). Walton’s argumentation scheme is related to our
problem of models of justification in the following ways. An argument following an
appropriate scheme provides presumptive justification for its conclusion, which can be
challenged through critical questions associated with the scheme. If a relevant challenge
cannot be adequately answered, the argument may lose its justificatory force. Whether a
challenge succeeds depends in part on the burden of proof governing the argumentative
exchange. Through a more socially and empirical grounded analysis of actual reasoning
patterns from the real world, Walton thus brings attention to the diverse, socially
situated ways in which people construct, challenge, and defend justifications.

2.8 Chapter summary

In this chapter, we reviewed a range of related work. Through this review, we end with
three issues that warrant an investigation into justifications of unreliability propagation.
First, determining whether a dependent entity is unreliable is often complex, as we see
from the cases of citations to retracted articles and papers utilizing misidentified cell
lines. The challenges partly come from information availability and findability and partly
from the complexity of knowledge production itself. Second, there is a lack of a clear
sense of what brings an entity over the threshold for scrutiny or sanction. Very little
guidance can be found in policies as well. In the reviewed technology development,
researchers define their own terms, operationalized in their algorithms or models. Lastly,
limited attention has been given to justifications that can be communicated to affected
authors and the public, either in computer-generated assessments or in actual decision
making. This tendency risks making such measures look authoritarian, without
adequately accounting for the aforementioned complexity of knowledge production.
   We have mentioned before that the term “unreliability propagation” was coined and
used as an umbrella term, or metaphor, for a range of cases. Prior researchers have used
terms such as “error propagation” or “error cascade,” but we cannot adopt the same
terminology because, in many cases, the entity in question is not erroneous but
unreliable. Moreover, we identified several textual examples from policy documents and
retraction notices that convey a very similar notion. Given the close relationship between
reliance and trust, as we reviewed before, the “unreliability” in unreliability propagation
is therefore not meaningless.
   Last but not least, we reviewed models of justification formation, where John
Pollock’s work on defeasible reasoning plays a central role. In the coming chapter, we will

3.1 The method of analysis

3.1.1 Analysis-by-decomposition

The analysis-by-decomposition approach, as the name suggests, decomposes a concept
into its constituents. We ask what must be true for a thing to fall under a concept, and
then try to identify all conditions explicitly. For example, a bachelor is an adult man
that is unmarried. So the conditions can be decomposed into:


                               𝑋 is a bachelor if and only if:

                               (i) 𝑋 is a man;

                               (ii) 𝑋 is an adult;

                               (ii) 𝑋 is unmarried.


   Each condition must be met to make 𝑋 a bachelor. In discussions of conceptual
analysis it is said that each condition is true if “X is a bachelor” is true and if all the
conditions are met then “𝑋 is a bachelor” is true, that is, these conditions are
individually necessary and collectively sufficient of X’s being a bachelor.

                                              31
   However, it is not always helpful to have the analysandum, the statement to be
analyzed, written as “𝑋 is {Concept Name} if and only if.” The analysis of the concept
of Knowledge serves an example. Instead of writing the statement as “𝑃 is knowledge if
and only if,” the more approachable alternative is “𝑆 knows 𝑃 if and only if,” which
allows our intuition to participate in the analysis. In epistemology the following is
referred to as the “standard” analysis of knowledge.


                          𝑆 knows 𝑃 if and only if:

                          (i) 𝑃 is true;

                          (ii) 𝑆 believes that 𝑃 ;

                          (iii) 𝑆 is justified in believing that 𝑃 .

An analysis can also be transformative, as an analyst leverages a more revealing
framework, such as logic or algebra, to aid their analysis. By using logic, we avoide being
misled by cues from natural language statements. An example is the analysis of the
negative existential statement as the following one.



                                  Unicorns do not exist.


   To understand what this statement means, we first decompose it into subject and
predicate, with “unicorns” as the subject and “do not exist” as the predicate. Based on
this treatment, we transform the statement into a formal logic form:


                                   ∀𝑥(𝑈 (𝑥) → ¬𝐸(𝑥)),


where

                                  𝑈 (𝑥) = 𝑥 is a unicorn.

                                     𝐸(𝑥) = 𝑥 exists.

                                   ∀𝑥(𝑈 (𝑥) → ¬𝐸(𝑥))

3.1.3 Counterexample construction and resolution

A counterexample is a case that can demonstrate the falsity or inadequacy of a claim,
definition, conjecture, or theory. In mathematics, counterexamples are useful because
they disprove false conjectures efficiently. For example, recently, Claude Fable came up
with a counterexample that disapproved the famous Jacobian conjecture (Sparkes, 2026).
   We use the Gettier cases and the subsequent patches to illustrate counterexample
construction and resolution in conceptual analysis. Gettier cases are counterexamples to
the JTB theory of knowledge, which is that knowledge is justified true belief. Gettier
constructed two counterexamples (or cases) that meet the justified true belief criteria,
but intuitively failed to count as knowledge (Gettier, 1963). We used the first Gettier



                                            33
counterexample as illustration. In this counterexample, the main character, Jones
believes that “the man who will get the job has 10 coins in his pocket.” Jones was
thinking of Smith, his colleague, who he has good evidence that (1) will get the job and
(2) has 10 coins in his pocket. However, it turned out Jones was the person who got the
job and he happened to have 10 coins in his pocket. This belief that “the man who will
get the job has ten coins in his pocket” is justified true belief but we would not say that
John knows that “the main who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket,” because he
was clearly thinking about Smith but not himself.
   In analysis-by-decomposition (or the classical conceptual analysis), the
counterexample can be useful in several ways. Most obviously, it points to the deficiency
of the conditions we proposed, for example, justified true belief is not sufficient for
knowledge. Second, it can be diagnostic by revealing what the analyst might have
overlooked. For example, we might say that Jones benefit from some kind of luck: that
although he was chosen instead of Smith, he happened to have 10 coins in his pocket as
well. The understanding that luck is at play here inspires additional conditions such as
Nozick’s counterfactual conditions on belief (Nozick, 1981). The counterfactual
conditions state that (1) were 𝑝 false, the subject would not believe 𝑝 and (2) were 𝑝
true, the subject would believe 𝑝 and help to rule out many luckily true Gettier cases.

proposed, and we review four of them below, centering on what conditions should be met
when the conditional “if 𝑃 then 𝑄” is true. In a conditional statement, 𝑃 is called the
antecedent and 𝑄 the consequent.

3.2.1 Material implication

   Material conditional can be quite counterintuitive. Yet counterintuitive examples help
us appreciate what it means for a conditional to be true. The following statement is true
despite both antecedent and consequent are false and lack any meaningful connection: “If
the Moon is made of cheese, then 2 + 2 = 5.” As a material conditional, this statement is
true because its antecedent is false, in which case, it does not matter whether the
consequent is true or false.

3.2.4 Counterfactual implication

A counterfactual implication can be written as:


                                          𝑃 2→ 𝑄

   𝑃 2→ 𝑄 is read as: “If it had been/were the case that 𝑃 , it would have been/would


                                             36
be the case that 𝑄” (Fenton-Glynn, 2021). 𝑃 2→ 𝑄 is true when among the closest
relevantly similar possible worlds to the actual world where 𝑃 is true, 𝑄 is also
true (D. K. Lewis, 1973). For example, the statement of “if we strike the match, it will
light” is evaluated in a world that is similar and relevant enough to the world where the
statement was made. It is not a world where oxygen is absent or the match is wet. In
comparison to the metaphysical implication, a conditional interpreted as a counterfactual
does not need to be true in every possible world for the conditional itself to be true.


Counterfactual dependence and causation            Lewis defined counterfactual
dependence as a pair of counterfactual conditionals (D. Lewis, 1973). Let 𝑐 and 𝑒 be two
token events and 𝑂(𝑐) and 𝑂(𝑒) be the propositions that 𝑐 occurs and 𝑒 occurs. 𝑒 is
counterfactual dependent on 𝑐 if and only if:




                                  (i)   𝑂(𝑐) 2→ 𝑂(𝑒).                                       (1)

                                  (ii) ¬𝑂(𝑐) 2→ ¬𝑂(𝑒).                                      (2)


   When the antecedent is true, Lewis’ possible worlds semantics guarantees that the
truth-value of the counterfactual conditional will be equivalent to the truth-value of the
consequent (𝑂(𝑒)) (Fenton-Glynn, 2021). Therefore, the truth-value of 𝑖 is true when
𝑂(𝑒) and 𝑂(𝑐) are true (i.e., both cause and effect occur in the actual world, despite
whether 𝑐 causes 𝑒). Therefore, when this is the case, (ii) does the substantive work of
establishing counterfactual dependence: it requires that 𝑒 would not have occurred had 𝑐
not occurred, which is the “but for” test for causation (𝑒 would not have happened but
for the occurrence of 𝑐), the dominant definition used in law for cause-in-effect (Moore,
2024). However, the necessity of condition (ii) for 𝑐 to qualify as a cause of 𝑒 has been
challenged by three classes of counterexamples: early preemption, late preemption, and
symmetric overdetermination; Lewis’s causal chain account accommodates early

                                             37
preemption by requiring causation to be a continuous chain of counterfactual
dependences between 𝑒 and 𝑜, but still faces difficulties with late preemption and
symmetric overdetermination (Fenton-Glynn, 2021).


3.3 Properties of counterfactual conditionals

Here we review a few important properties of the counterfactual conditional.


The contraposition of counterfactual conditionals Contraposition is the inference
pattern that reverses a conditional and negates both parts. For example:


                                          𝑃 →𝑄

   It’s contraposition is:


                                        ¬𝑄 → ¬𝑃

   In classical propositional logic, a material conditional and its contrapositive are
logically equivalent and therefore share the same truth value.


                                   𝑃 → 𝑄 ≡ ¬𝑄 → ¬𝑃.

   However, a counterfactual conditional being true does not in general validate its
contrapositive. We illustrate this property using the following example.


                                𝑃 ∶= Electricity goes out.

                                𝑄 ∶= Dinner is late.

   Here, 𝑃 and 𝑄 are in the present tense but should be treated as abstract
propositional descriptions; we do not commit them to a particular time.
   Now we write out the Would conditional in both formal notations and natural


                                            38
language. As a reminder, the Would conditional of “if 𝑃 then 𝑄” is read as “if 𝑃 were
the case, then 𝑄 would be the case.” In the if-clause part, we used past subjunctive tense
(“were to go”) to show a hypothetical or imaginary event. And in the ‘main clause (after
“then”), we used present conditional tense (“would be”) for an hypothetical situation.




       𝐶 ∶= 𝑃 2→ 𝑄              If electricity were to go out, dinner would be late.


   Now we convert formal notation into a natural language sentence, which is:




     𝐶 ∗ ∶= ¬𝑄 2→ ¬𝑃           If dinner were not later, electricity would not go out.


   However, this 𝐶 ∗ sounds unnatural. The prospective reading of were to invites us to
imagine a change in what happens with dinner and then consider what would follow from
that change. Yet changing whether dinner is on time would not ordinarily alter whether
the electricity goes out. This oddity reflects the temporal asymmetry of counterfactual
conditionals: unless the temporal order is explicitly specified, we normally interpret the
antecedent as preceding the consequent.
   Therefore, we will need to use conditional perfect tense to reflect a reversed temporal
order: the consequent proceeds the antecedent. The revised 𝐶 ∗ is:




   𝐶 ∗ ∶= ¬𝑄 2→ ¬𝑃         If dinner were not late, electricity would not have gone out.


   Most people would find 𝐶 more plausible than 𝐶 ∗ , suggesting that they need not
share the same truth value; that is, counterfactual conditionals do not generally
contrapose. The theoretical reason traces back to how we evaluate the truth values of


                                            39
counterfactual conditionals (see Section 3.2). 𝐶 is evaluated with respect to the closest
possible worlds in which 𝑃 is true, that is, worlds in which the electricity goes out. We
ask: among the possible worlds most similar to the actual world in which the electricity
goes out, is dinner late? The answer is probably yes.7 However, when evaluating 𝐶 ∗ , we
ask: among the possible worlds most similar to the actual world in which dinner is not
late, did the electricity stay on? Here, we can plausibly imagine nearby worlds in which
dinner is not late even though the electricity goes out, as well as worlds in which dinner
is not late because the electricity stays on. Thus, the closest 𝑃 -worlds relevant to 𝐶 need
not align with the closest ¬𝑄-worlds relevant to 𝐶 ∗ .


The transitivity of counterfactual conditionals If a relationship 𝑅 is transitive on
the set 𝑋, for element 𝑎, 𝑏, and 𝑐 in 𝑋, we have: if 𝑎𝑅𝑏 and 𝑏𝑅𝑐, then 𝑎𝑅𝑐.
    Counterfactual conditionals are generally not transitive. Below is an example.




𝐶1 ∶= 𝑃 2→ 𝑄          If today were freezing cold, Mary would be wearing a puffer jacket.

𝐶2 ∶= 𝑄 2→ 𝑅          If Mary were wearing a puffer jacket today, she would be uncomfortably warm.

𝐶3 ∶= 𝑃 2→ 𝑅          If today were freezing cold, Mary would be uncomfortably warm.


    𝐶1 and 𝐶2 are likely true, but 𝐶3 is intuitively false, and therefore transitivity fails.
The explanation is as follows based on the Stalnaker–Lewis semantics for
counterfactuals (D. K. Lewis, 1973; Small, 1982). When we evaluate 𝐶1 , we examine
whether 𝑄 is true in the closest relevantly similar possible worlds where 𝑃 is true (i.e.,
worlds in which today is freezing cold), and find—given Mary’s dressing habits—that 𝑄
is true. However, when we evaluate 𝐶2 , we examine whether 𝑅 is true in the closest
relevantly similar possible worlds where 𝑄 is true (i.e., worlds much like today,

    7
      Admittedly, this may become less plausible with the increasing convenience of takeout. Dinner would
well be earlier if electricity were to go out.


                                                   40
presumably a warm or hot day, except that Mary wears a puffer jacket), and find—given
ordinary human physiology—that 𝑅 is true. Crucially, these closest 𝑄-worlds are not the
𝑄-worlds reached in evaluating 𝐶1 : in the latter, Mary wears a puffer jacket on a
freezing-cold day. Thus, although 𝑄 is true in the closest 𝑃 -worlds, the background
conditions under which 𝑄 makes 𝑅 true have changed. Consequently, 𝐶3 does not follow.
      Although in general counterfactual conditionals are not transitive, an important
theoretical issue is when transitivity is valid. One special case is limited
transitivity (“Debates over Counterfactual Principles”, 2025), which is formally expressed
as:


                              𝑃 2→ 𝑄, (𝑃 ∧ 𝑄) 2→ 𝑅∴𝑃 2→ 𝑅

4.1 The pre-analytical intuition of unreliability propagation

Unreliability propagation exists in our intuition. For example, it is very natural for us to
think that if a test is unreliable than the result from the test is unreliable. Or, if a
witness if unreliable, their testimony is unreliable. Motion of unreliability propagation
also appears in scientific writings. For example, in an account of the economic losses
resulting from cell misidentification, the author wrote that “Cell line misidentification ...
now compromises roughly one in five lines and renders thousands of papers potentially
unreliable, propagating unreliable data through hundreds of thousands of
citations” (Weiskirchen, 2026). This “if something is unreliable, then another thing is
unreliable” is our pre-analytical intuition of unreliability propagation.

Material implication is not a candidate.            If we treat “if 𝑥 is unreliable, then 𝑦 is

situation remains. Thus, UP-2-Mat amounts to requiring that 𝑥 and 𝑦 are both

unreliability of 𝑥 and 𝑦 may be entirely unrelated.

Metaphysical (broadly logical) implication is not a candidate but the more

   Unfortunately, UP-2-CF-ii has the same truth conditions as a material conditional
due to its interaction with UP-2-CF-i. The reason is as follows. Under Lewis’s strong
centering principle, when U(𝑥) is true in the actual world, UP-2-CF-ii is true if and only
if U(𝑦) is also true in the actual world, because the actual world is more similar to itself
than any other possible worlds, so the truth of U(𝑦) must hold in the actual world, and
once this is established, the truth of the conditional is also established. Consequently,
given UP-2-CF-i, UP-2-CF-ii does not establish any connection between the unreliability
of 𝑥 and that of 𝑦, as jointly, they only establish that both 𝑥 and 𝑦 are unreliable.

the second counterfactual should use ¬U(𝑥) and ¬U(𝑦): an entity may occupy an
intermediate status, satisfying neither the criteria for being reliable nor those for being
unreliable. Nevertheless, expressing the counterfactual in terms of reliability makes it
easier for us humans to evaluate concrete cases. We therefore use R(𝑥) and R(𝑦) instead,
provisionally treating reliability and unreliability as complementary.


                               Candidate A: U(𝑦) 2→ U(𝑥)

UP-2-CF-i and UP-2-CF-ii have already guaranteed U(𝑦), which means U(𝑦) and U(𝑥)

converse behave like a material conditional and is guaranteed to be true.
   Candidate B, the inverse of UP-2-CF-ii U(𝑥) 2→ U(𝑦) provides a strong
support of unreliability propagation from 𝑥 to 𝑦, but is too demanding.
Candidate B and UP-2-CF-ii together form the counterfactual dependence, as we
discussed in Section 3.2.4. If candidate B is true, this offer strong support to track
unreliability propagation from 𝑥 to 𝑦: We observe in all closest possible worlds where 𝑥 is
reliable, and we found that 𝑦 is also reliable.
   However, Candidate B is too demanding. Consider the following case: Suppose 𝑦 uses
a flawed method 𝑥, but also contains affected by another unreliable source 𝑧. Even if 𝑥
were reliable, 𝑦 would still be unreliable because of 𝑧. Candidate B would therefore be
false, even though 𝑥’s unreliability has contributed to 𝑦’s unreliability.
   There is also cases where B is true but unreliability has not propagated from 𝑥 to 𝑦.

Consider the following cases. We have two papers 𝑥 and 𝑦 using the same method 𝑧. The
method 𝑧 made 𝑥 unreliable, and it does the same to 𝑦. We observe in the closest
possible worlds where 𝑥 is reliable (by remedying 𝑧), we also discover that 𝑦 is also
reliable. So Candidate B is true, but unreliability has not propagated from 𝑥 to 𝑦.
   Given the connection between counterfactual dependence and causation, we should
also make the remark that we need not establish that 𝑥’s unreliability is a cause of 𝑦’s
unreliability. Consider the following case: A scientist knows that paper 𝑥 is unreliable
but nevertheless decides to use its main findings to support a key argument in their
paper 𝑦. In this case, we may recognize that unreliability has propagated from 𝑥 to 𝑦, yet
it seems more natural to attribute 𝑦’s unreliability to the scientist’s practice than to
identify 𝑥’s unreliability as its cause.
   Candidate C, the contrapositive R(𝑦) 2→ R(𝑥), is not a candidate We have
discussed in Section 3.2.4 that the contrapositive of a true counterfactual conditional is
not necessarily true, so Candidate C is not redundant. But there is an issue with
Candidate C. Consider the dataset 𝑥 is unreliable because some of its instances were
mislabeled, making the machine learning model 𝑦 produced by researchers unreliable.
Yet in closest possible worlds, researchers discovered the issue and corrected the labels,
making 𝑦 reliable while leaving 𝑥 still unreliable, and therefore making C false despite the
fact that unreliability has propagated.
   Our analysis suggests that counterfactual conditionals failed to provide necessary and
sufficient conditions for unreliability propagation. Neither Candidate B nor Candidate C
is necessary, as we showed in cases where unreliability may propagate from 𝑥 to 𝑦 even
when either is false. Nor are they sufficient, since the reliability statuses of 𝑥 and 𝑦 may
vary together across possible worlds without unreliability propagating from one to the
other. Nevertheless, Candidate B, R(𝑥) 2→ R(𝑦), remains useful. When it is true, it may
provide a defeasible reason to believe that unreliability has propagated from 𝑥 to 𝑦.

This chapter presented a conceptual analysis aimed at identifying the individually
necessary and jointly sufficient conditions for unreliability to propagate from an entity 𝑥
to an entity 𝑦, irrespective of their types. We developed UP-1 from our basic
pre-analytical intuition about unreliability propagation. Its first condition Up-1-i states
that 𝑥 is unreliable, and its second condition Up-1-ii states that if 𝑥 is unreliable, then 𝑦
is unreliable. Drawing on conditional logic and analysis-by-transformation, we
investigated four interpretations of the second condition: material implication, logical
implication, metaphysical implication, and counterfactual implication, for version two of
our analysis (UP-2).
   Material implication is not a candidate because, together with the first condition, it
requires only that 𝑥 and 𝑦 are both unreliable, without establishing any relationship
between their unreliability. Logical implication remains a possible candidate, provided
that we can specify and justify a background theory Γ under which U(𝑥) entails U(𝑦).
The metaphysical interpretation is too strong for the context-dependent nature of
unreliability propagation. However, an alteration of the conditional revealed an
enthymematic argument whose implicit premise concerns the requirements an evaluator
imposes when judging 𝑦 to be reliable. These implicit requirements resemble the
assumptions contained in Γ, although they concern epistemic judgment rather than
logical entailment. Finally, our investigation of counterfactual implication failed to yield
a set of necessary and sufficient conditions for unreliability propagation. Nevertheless,
Candidate B, R(𝑥) 2→ R(𝑦), remains potentially useful, as its truth may form a
defeasible justification for judging that unreliability has propagated from 𝑥 to 𝑦. In the
next chapter, we will apply the insights from this analysis to the
Willoughby–Jansma–Hoye (WJH) protocol code glitch case.

5.1 Case background

In late 2019, a paper published in Organic Letters (Neupane et al., 2019) reported a code
glitch in the widely used Willoughby–Jansma–Hoye (WJH) computational chemistry
protocol for calculating nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) chemical shifts. The study
found that the protocol’s Python scripts produced different results depending on the
operating system (Windows, macOS, or Linux). Further investigation traced the issue to
Python Script D, which calculates Boltzmann-weighted average chemical shifts using two
types of output files for each conformer (a possible spatial arrangement of a molecule):
an NMR file containing calculated chemical shifts (e.g., 1b-nmr-conf-1.out) and a
frequency/optimization (freq/opt) file containing information needed to determine its
Boltzmann weight (e.g., 1b-opt_freq-conf-1.out). To calculate the weighted average
correctly, Script D must match the NMR and freq/opt files belonging to the same
conformer. However, the 2014 version of Script D relied on the order in which the Python
function glob.glob() returned the files, assuming that the NMR and freq/opt files of
the same conformer would occupy corresponding positions in their respective lists. This
assumption failed on some operating systems, including Ubuntu 16 and macOS Mojave,
because the order of files returned by glob.glob() is not guaranteed. Consequently, the
script could pair the NMR file of one conformer with the freq/opt file of another,
producing incorrect results. A 2020 addendum to the original protocol (Willoughby
et al., 2020) provided a corrected version of Script D that explicitly matched files using
the conformer identifiers in their filenames. The WJH protocol was described as “one of
the most read and highly cited works in the field” (Marcarino et al., 2020). The discovery
therefore raised concerns about the potential effects of the code glitch on previously
published research and attracted media attention, prompting headlines such as “A Code
Glitch May Have Caused Errors in More Than 100 Published Studies” (Bender, 2019).




                                            53
5.2 Why study this case?

This case originates in chemistry, where my domain expertise proved especially useful.
The source of unreliability is relatively straightforward: it is localized within a single
component of the protocol, namely, Python Script D. Moreover, the issue is readily
communicable to computer scientists, information scientists, and other non-chemists, as
it requires only minimal domain knowledge to grasp: At a high level, it suffices to
understand that the WJH protocol consists of multiple steps, and that the unreliability is
confined to just one of them.


5.3 Dataset curation

5.3.1 Collecting the publications and metadata

On July 6, 2023, we identified publications citing Willoughby et al., 2014 and retrieved
the metadata of 277 citing publications from Web of Science (WoS) and 285 citing
publications from Scopus. After merging the dataset and removing duplicate items, 286
citing publications remained. Later, we removed 2 publications that are not in English,
leaving 284 citing publications in our final dataset (Zheng, Fu, Vandel, & Schneider,
2024). HZ downloaded all publications’ PDFs for me (YF) to annotate.


5.3.2 Annotation by first chemistry expert

I served as the first chemistry expert. I used publication metadata and full texts to assess
the risk of unreliability propagating from the WJH protocol to citing publications. I first
examined how the WJH protocol was cited in each publication. Based on the citation
context, I inferred the significance of the WJH protocol to the citing publication and
made an initial determination of the risk of unreliability propagation. I then sought
counter-evidence to challenge this determination, drawing on publication metadata and
other information from the full text. For each publication, I recorded my answers to four
questions: (1) Does the paper state in the citation context that it used Willoughby–Hoye

                                              54
protocol script D? (2) Can the citation context rule out the possibility that the authors
used the original Willoughby–Jansma–Hoye protocol script D? (3) Is there other evidence
that rules out the possibility that the publication was affected by the code glitch? (4)
Considering all available evidence, can the possibility that the publication was affected
by the code glitch be ruled out? I recorded a ‘Yes” or ‘No” answer to each question,
supplemented by notes in the annotation spreadsheet to explain my reasoning. The final
annotation was a binary classification: ‘at risk” or ‘not at risk” (of unreliability
propagation). The annotation took me about 40+ hours to complete.


5.3.3 Enriching the dataset with manually extracted citation contexts

Given the significance of citation context in my annotation process, we decided to extract
citation contexts from those publications. HZ manually extracted citation contexts of

the introduction section in the process. To be more specific, a citation context is a text
(typically a clause, one or more sentences, or a paragraph) Small, 1982 in a scientific
publication that contains a reference to the citation. Figure 5.1 shows an example.

   Step-scoped citation is the largest category with 96 publications (34%). Step-scoped

of citation to imply risk of unreliability propagation, in absence of other counter-evidence.

reasonable to believe that unreliability has propagated to the paper than to withhold

5.5 Chapter summary

In this chapter, I introduced the Willoughby–Jansma–Hoye (WJH) code glitch case and
described the curation of a dataset of 284 publications citing the WJH protocol. Each
publication was assigned an expert verdict of “at risk” or “not at risk” of unreliability
propagation. I annotated all 284 publications, and a second chemistry expert annotated
a representative sample of 77. Interannotator agreement, measured by Cohen’s kappa,
rose from 0.34 to 0.70 after the two annotators reconciled their disagreements. A
reconciliation policy was used to settle the remaining cases.
   I categorized the publications based on how they cited the protocol. Publications
citing the WJH protocol for the Boltzmann-weighted averaging step or the protocol as a
whole were deemed at elevated risk, although this risk could be canceled by
counter-evidence, such as evidence that the authors were aware of the code glitch.
Background citations, review articles, and citations to steps other than
Boltzmann-weighted averaging were used as evidence for a “not at risk” label. However,
the second expert sometimes inferred that authors might have used additional steps of
the protocol beyond those explicitly cited, leading to different risk assessments.
   The binary labels obscure differences in the degree of risk within the “at risk” group.

understood as a silver standard. The dataset and these observations provide the
empirical basis for the chapters that follow.

6.3 Computational pipelines

Using our dataset (Zheng, Fu, Vandel, & Schneider, 2024), we implemented a prototype
computational pipeline in Python to connect the information documented in the datasets
to the defeaters described above and generate publication-level verdict and justifications.
   The pipeline first addresses data quality issues in the two source files. The metadata
file, Willoughby2014_286citing_publications.csv, contains the metadata (e.g.,

                                            66
authors, title, journal name, etc.) of the 286 publications, with DOI as the primary key.
The citation context file, Willoughby2014_citation_contexts.csv, contains 401
citation context occurrences across 284 publications that exclude the two
Chinese-language articles. It also contains answers to five questions (listed below) which
are pertinent to risk assessment.

  • Is it a review according to Scopus? (retrieval date: November 25, 2023)

  • Is it a review according to WoS? (retrieval date: November 25, 2023)

  • Do at least one of Neupane et al., 2019 or Willoughby et al., 2020 appear in the
     bibliography according to Scopus?

  • Do at least one of Neupane et al., 2019 or Willoughby et al., 2020 appear in the
     bibliography according to WoS?

  • Does the introduction section contain all the citations to Willoughby et al., 2014?

   The citation context file contains more than the citation contexts data. It repeats
publication-level attributes (five questions plus others) alongside cc-level attributes, and
these repeated attributes depend on DOI alone. This structure admits data
inconsistency: 13 publications had conflicting values for column “Does the introduction
section contain all the citations to Willoughby et al., 2014?”. To mitigate the issue, the
pipeline extracts a separate publication-level table from the citation context file and
excludes the 13 publications with inconsistencies. It also standardizes DOI capitalization
and removes surrounding whitespace to prepare the linkage with the metadata file. We
also excludes the two Chinese-language articles records without corresponding citation
contexts, leaving the cleaned file with 271 publications.
   The resulting records are joined with the metadata file. The pipeline then constructs
indicators of authors’ awareness of the code glitch, review status, and introduction-only
citations. Awareness is inferred when either Scopus or Web of Science records a citation

                                             67
to the paper reporting the code glitch or the addendum. Similarly, a publication is
treated as a review if either database identifies it as such. Introduction-only status is
taken from the corresponding annotation. For each satisfied condition, the pipeline
generates a template-based justification identifying the publication, judging it “not at
risk,” and stating the reason based on the formulations above. When multiple conditions
are satisfied, all corresponding justifications are retained, which differs from the
treatment in Zheng, Fu, Sarol, et al. (2024). When none is satisfied, the publication
remains judged “at risk”: the evidence examined supplies no identified reason to defeat
the inference from citation to UP. This default judgment is itself defeasible, since
additional evidence, including the content of citation contexts, may warrant revising it.
   We present each assessment as an assessment card to make its reasoning clear and
readable. Each card identifies the publication and presents its justifications and final
verdict in natural language, alongside a visualization of the reasoning using formal
notation. The justification messages and verdict are read directly from the pipeline’s
CSV output, chpt6-results.csv, and the cards are generated as individual PNG files.
The verdict is displayed simply as “at risk” or “not at risk,” while the justification
messages state the grounds for the judgment and acknowledge defeasibility where
applicable. The visualization shows which inference is undercut; when no defeater is
identified, it shows the default inference remaining undefeated.
   Figures 6.1–6.5 illustrate five outputs. The first three isolate the contribution of each
defeater: Liu et al. (2020) satisfies the awareness condition alone, Jonas et al. (2022) the
review condition alone, and Lomas (2014) the introduction-only condition alone.
Awareness undercuts the inference from citation to UP, 𝑟1𝑎 , whereas review status and
introduction-only citations undercut the inference from citation to use, 𝑟2𝑎 . The
introduction-only justification explicitly acknowledges that citation text indicating actual
use could defeat the reason supplied by citation location. Matsuo et al. (2017) illustrates
the default judgment when none of the three indicators is positive. Finally, Kim et al.


                                             68
(2020) illustrates how the pipeline retains both awareness and review justifications for one
publication. These justifications challenge different steps in the reasoning; retaining both
makes the grounds for the verdict explicit without assigning a higher confidence score.
